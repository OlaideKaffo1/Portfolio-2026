I was the only designer on this, and I led it end to end, from research and strategy to design and the front-end build. I worked with our Head of Product and one engineer, with input from our coaching and customer teams. It took about six weeks in spring 2026, and it's live. The short version is that we set out to build an AI assistant, and I made the case to ship a three-question Playbook Recommender first. New customers starting a project and a playbook went from 57% to 85%.

About 43% of new customers never started a project. They came with a real business problem, found a library of playbooks, and couldn't tell which one was right for them. Each playbook takes hours, like running customer interviews or testing a price, so picking the wrong one wastes that time. People hesitated, guessed or gave up. For Strategyzer this was the costliest place to lose people. If nobody starts a playbook, nobody gets value, and every number from sign-up to renewal depends on that first step. How people found their first playbook depended on the kind of customer they were. Enterprise customers had a coach who asked two or three questions and said start here. Self-serve customers got the whole library and chose alone, just when they knew the least.

Even our own coaches found choosing a playbook hard.

It was the most common complaint in every conversation. So the problem was in the product.

I interviewed eight customers, plus our customer success and coaching teams. They fell into two groups. Solo founders were testing an idea before pitching investors, and were new to Strategyzer's terms. Heads of strategy and innovation were testing an idea before presenting to senior leaders, often with half the work already done. Very different people with the same problem. Too many ideas, no clear first step, and an important meeting coming up. In every conversation, the most common complaint was choosing a playbook, and even our own coaches said it. If our coaches found it hard, the problem was in the product. Returning customers faced the same choice every time they started something new.

Customers were asking for AI, so we started there.

Strattie AI

An assistant across the whole platform, like a Strategyzer coach beside you. I designed it and built a working prototype with Claude.

  • A coach you could chat with
  • Help on the canvas
  • Playbook recommendations, one job of many
Customers were asking for AI, so we started there. The first idea was ambitious. Strattie AI was an assistant across the whole platform, like having a Strategyzer coach beside you at every step. I designed it and built a working prototype with Claude, using FondUI, our design system. It could chat as a coach on every page, help on the canvas by summarising work, pointing to evidence, checking ideas and drafting sticky notes, and recommend playbooks as one of its many jobs. The video goes from "I'm not sure who my customers are" to a finished workspace. It's fully prototyped, but it wasn't in the first release.

Building it for real taught us three things a static design would have hidden. First, the value was in the questions. A good coach asks two or three questions before recommending anything. In an open chat, the assistant often answered before it had asked them, and you can see it here, where one message is enough for it to recommend a playbook. Second, doing it well was a much bigger job than it looked. Vague requests, a changing library and knowing when to say "I don't know" were more than our small team could take on at the time. Third, chat is the wrong tool for "where do I start?". People came to us because they couldn't put their problem into words, and a chat box asks them to do exactly that.

Engineering couldn't build the full assistant in the time we needed. The team's first instinct was a smaller chatbot, and that was a fair instinct. I pushed back, because a smaller chatbot is just a worse chatbot. We'd still have to design the whole conversation. It could still recommend playbooks that don't exist. People would still have to type a problem they couldn't put into words. And it wouldn't clearly tell us whether guidance helps. A few fixed questions solve all four.

So I changed the question

Decision

Ship the Recommender first. The assistant comes next.

So I changed the question. Of everything the assistant could do, one job came first every time, and that was finding the right playbook. It's where every new project starts, and where people kept getting stuck. I needed to convince our CEO and our Head of Product, who was keen on the assistant. On a call, I showed them the working prototype and my research. Every group we spoke to had the same need, help choosing where to start. The pushback was that customers were asking for AI, and guided questions could look like we were falling behind. My answer was that I wasn't against AI, and the platform will need it, but choosing a playbook comes first. If people get stuck there, they'll leave before they ever see an AI feature. Fix that first, and every AI feature we add later reaches people who are already getting value. The decision was to ship the Recommender first, with the assistant next.

A trade-off, made knowingly.

I'd rather ship one job done well than several half-done.

It was a trade-off, and I made it knowingly. It bought us four things. It can only recommend playbooks that exist. It's honest when nothing fits, and says so instead of guessing. It takes three taps instead of explaining a problem you can't yet put into words. And it proved guidance helps before we invested in the full assistant. It cost us the full assistant, help on the canvas, which our coaches had asked for, the chat panel and the onboarding tour, all moved to a later release. That was hard to let go of. I'd rather ship one job done well than several half-done.

I tried three shapes as working flows. A pop-up in the middle of the screen covered the library people were choosing from. A tall side panel looked like a chat that wasn't there. A small panel in the corner won, because it was easy to find and the page stayed usable behind it. Answers shrink as you go, a pattern borrowed from Asana's AI menu. Once you pick an answer, it shrinks to a short message and the next question appears below, so the panel stays short and easy to read. I also dropped the tour-style version I explored first, with its progress bar and big welcome screen. This is a tool people return to whenever they're stuck, so it shouldn't feel like a one-time tour.

It also found a gap

No playbook helped people test whether customers want their offer. The playbook team started on one straight away.

Behind the questions is a map I built in two days from how customers described their situations. It has 5 problem areas, 17 goals and 45 paths, leading to 19 playbooks. Every path is mapped by hand in a working file, with each question, its answers and the playbook it leads to. Because it's written down and not generated by AI, we can trust it and fix it when it's wrong. The questions use everyday words, so founders new to Strategyzer can answer them. Question three, "where are you now?", only appears when it changes the answer, so people with work already done can skip ahead. Mapping every need also showed a gap. Nothing helped people test whether customers want their offer, which many customers came to us needing. The Recommender says so instead of guessing, and the team that writes playbooks started on one straight away.

When nothing fits, it says so.

It shows the closest options instead of guessing. The hardest call in the project.

Three more details that build trust

The panel stays the same sizeNo jumping as answers change.
Change an answer without starting overClick any earlier answer to edit it.
"Something else" in your own wordsAI finds the closest real playbooks.
The hardest call in the project was what to do when nothing fits. When there's no good match, the Recommender says so and shows the closest options side by side, instead of pretending one is right. Three smaller details build trust too. The panel keeps one height, because early versions grew and shrank with every answer and felt jumpy. You can click any earlier answer to edit it without starting over. And if none of the options fit, people pick "Something else" and type their situation. AI reads it and finds the closest playbooks from the same map, so it only suggests real ones, and it explains its pick in plain language. There's no fake thinking either. Questions appear instantly, with a short pause only before the recommendation.

A recommendation is only useful if people act on it. Inside a project, one click on "Add to this project" adds the playbook and starts it. In the library, the results filter what's shown to the recommended playbooks. From stuck to started takes about fifteen seconds, and that's the whole point of the project.

The team now works at least twice as fast.

Because I built it in code, people used the real thing instead of reacting to pictures of it. We ran about 24 sessions with coaches, customer teams and customers, about eight each. The biggest change was rewriting the questions so they were easier to understand, and so no one worried about picking the wrong answer. I designed this in code, not Figma. I built the front end myself with Claude on FondUI, and that code went straight into the product with no handover and no rebuild. Our engineer built everything behind it, which means data, tracking and connecting it to the rest of the platform. Automated tests clicked through every step and caught things that looked right but didn't work. Research took one to two weeks, the assistant prototype a few days, the question map two days, the Recommender three weeks to build, test and refine, and launch about a week and a half. About six weeks in total. The team now works at least twice as fast.

We measured over four months, comparing the months before and after launch. Product data for getting started and coming back, customer surveys and the customer success team for satisfaction. New customers starting a project and a playbook went from 57% to 85%. Satisfaction went from 68% to 95%. People returning within 30 days went from about 10% to 40%, four times as many, and most come back weekly. That's the one that says the most about the design. With the right playbook from the start, people had a reason to return. And about 18% of customers who had cancelled came back to try the Recommender within two weeks of the release email. These results belong to the product and the team.
