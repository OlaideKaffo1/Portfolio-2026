Solving the first-step problem

We set out to build an AI assistant. I made the case to ship a three-question Playbook Recommender first, and it moved every number that mattered.

RoleSenior product designer, and the only designer
TeamHead of Product, one engineer, coaching and customer teams
TimelineAbout 6 weeks, spring 2026
StatusLive
I was the only designer on this. I led it end to end, from research and strategy to design and the front-end build. I worked with our Head of Product and one engineer, with input from our coaching and customer teams. It took about six weeks in spring 2026, and it's live. The short version is that we set out to build an AI assistant, and I made the case to ship a three-question Playbook Recommender first.

About 43% of new customers never started a project. They came with a real business problem, found the playbook library, and couldn't tell which one was right for them. Each playbook takes hours, like running customer interviews or testing a price, so picking the wrong one wastes that time. People hesitated, guessed or gave up. For Strategyzer this was the costliest place to lose people. If nobody starts a playbook, nobody gets value, and every number from sign-up to renewal depends on that first step. Enterprise customers had a coach who asked two or three questions and said start here. Self-serve customers got the whole library and had to choose alone.

One job came first every time. Finding the right playbook is where every new project starts, and where people kept getting stuck.

Engineering couldn't build the full assistant in the time we needed. The team's first instinct was a smaller chatbot, and that was a fair instinct. I pushed back, because a smaller chatbot is just a worse chatbot. We'd still have to design the whole conversation, it could still recommend playbooks that don't exist, people would still have to type a problem they couldn't put into words, and it wouldn't clearly tell us whether guidance helps. So I changed the question from "what's the smallest assistant we can build?" to "what's the one job that matters most right now?". The answer every time was finding the right playbook.

Making the case

I took the prototype and my research to our CEO and Head of Product.

Our Head of Product was keen on the assistant. On a call, I showed them the working prototype and my research. Every group we spoke to needed the same thing, help choosing where to start.

The pushback

Customers were asking for AI, and a set of guided questions could look like we were falling behind.

My answer

I wasn't against AI. The platform will need it. But choosing a playbook comes first. If people get stuck there, they'll leave before they ever see an AI feature. Fix that first, and every AI feature we add later reaches people who are already getting value.

Decision

Ship the Recommender first. The assistant comes next.

I needed to convince our CEO and our Head of Product, who was keen on the assistant. On a call, I showed them the working prototype and my research. My answer was that I wasn't against AI, and the platform will need it, but choosing a playbook comes first. If people get stuck there, they'll leave before they ever see an AI feature. Fix that first, and every AI feature we add later reaches people who are already getting value. The decision was to ship the Recommender first, with the assistant next.

It was a trade-off, and I made it knowingly.

Designing the Recommender · decision 6

Four small details that build trust.

The panel stays the same sizeIt keeps one height, and the content scrolls inside. Early versions jumped with every answer.
Change an answer without starting overClick any earlier answer to edit it.
AI for everything elsePick "Something else" and type your situation. AI finds the closest playbooks from the same map, so it only suggests real ones.
Honest when nothing fitsIt says so and shows the closest options side by side. The hardest call in the project.
Small details decided whether people trusted it. The panel stays the same size. Early versions grew and shrank with every answer, which felt jumpy, so now it keeps one height and the content scrolls inside. You can change an answer without starting over. If none of the options fit, people pick "Something else" and type their situation, and AI finds the closest playbooks from the same map, so it only suggests real ones and explains its pick in plain language. And when there's no good match, it says so and shows the closest options side by side instead of pretending one is right. That was the hardest call in the project. There's also no fake thinking. Questions appear instantly, with a short pause only before the recommendation.

We measured over four months, comparing the months before and after launch. Product data for getting started and coming back, customer surveys and the customer success team for satisfaction. New customers starting a project and a playbook went from 57% to 85%. Satisfaction went from 68% to 95%. People returning within 30 days went from about 10% to 40%, four times as many, and most come back weekly. That's the one that says the most about the design. With the right playbook from the start, people had a reason to return. And about 18% of customers who had cancelled came back to try the Recommender within two weeks of the release email. These results belong to the product and the team.
