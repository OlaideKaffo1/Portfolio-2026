## Feedback that went nowhere *(was "The problem")*

Average employee turnover had risen 28% in two years **[? source]**, and 65% of exit interviews named "unaddressed workplace frustrations" as a reason for leaving **[? source]**. Every company collected feedback. Almost none of it turned into change.

The problem looked different depending on who you asked.

> "I feel like my voice isn't really heard, as I don't see any changes in my department, even though I've given feedback and suggestions several times."
> *Software Engineer, mid-sized company*

> "My team is struggling, and I don't feel like I'm doing my best as a manager. I can't turn their feedback into action plans, because I don't know what's causing the friction."
> *Team Lead, customer success*

> "I've spent three months trying to get executive buy-in for our improvement plan, because I can't show data on its impact."
> *HR Leader, manufacturing company*

**Three people, one broken loop.** Employees spoke up, managers couldn't prioritise, and HR couldn't prove value, so nothing moved.

### Many complaints, four causes

| Cause | What it meant |
|---|---|
| **Trust gap** | People didn't believe honest feedback would lead to change. |
| **Feedback fatigue** | Long surveys, with nothing visible coming out of them. |
| **Visibility problem** | Managers found out about issues only once they were already critical. |
| **Action paralysis** | HR had the data but couldn't turn it into priorities. |

**Underneath it all:** feedback was treated as a survey to run, not a loop to close.

**Who it was for:** employees, who want to be heard without extra work; managers, who want to know what to fix first; and HR partners, who want a clear story that drives decisions and proves impact.

## One loop to close *(was "The idea")*

Every piece of feedback should lead somewhere visible. **[? confirm]**

**Four principles** **[? confirm or adjust]**:
1. **Quick to give.** Feedback takes a minute, not a morning.
2. **Clear what to fix first.** Managers see priorities, not raw data.
3. **Visible follow-through.** Employees see what changed because they spoke up.
4. **Impact you can prove.** HR can show leadership the return.

## A year later: what was still slow *(the turning point)*

After a year of real use, the data showed where the work was still slow. The feedback was coming in, but HR teams were drowning in it:
- Feedback was scattered across 5 to 8 platforms.
- 73% of HR leaders spent more time analysing data than acting on it.
- On average, 6 weeks passed between an issue starting and anyone spotting it.

I ran a second round of research over three weeks: 18 HR directors and VPs, 12 People Operations managers, 9 team leads and 4 C-suite executives.

> "I feel like I'm always putting out fires instead of preventing them. By the time we see patterns in our data, the damage is already done."
> *People Leader, manufacturing company*

That was a real need, and a business opportunity, which is why we built Fount AI.

## Act 2: Fount AI, an AI business partner

**Three strategic calls** (in the same style as "A strategic call" on the Strategyzer page):

1. **Help people decide, don't decide for them.** HR is accountable for its decisions, so full automation was the wrong tool. Every recommendation shows a confidence level, the reasoning behind it, and a way to override it.
2. **Answer "what should I do next?", not just "what's happening?"** People kept asking for next steps rather than charts. Recommendations come in three tiers: immediate actions, short-term strategies and long-term initiatives.
3. **A conversation, not another dashboard.** **[? the real reason. The PDF only says "research showed it's the best experience". For example: HR already juggled six dashboards, so the AI had to be a place to ask questions, not a seventh screen to read.]**
