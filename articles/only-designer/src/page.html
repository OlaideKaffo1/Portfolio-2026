The Only Designer in the Room

How I scaled design across sales, marketing and client delivery.

Olaide · Senior product designer at StrategyzerSep 26, 2026

A few months ago, a sales deck at Strategyzer took three to four days to get right. Today a rep can go from finished content to a polished, on-brand deck in about thirty minutes.

3–4 days→About 30 minutesTo turn finished content into a sales deck

That change didn't come from new hires or outside help. It came from a set of Claude skills I built that give our sales, marketing and client delivery teams direct access to our design system, including the decisions a designer would normally make for them.

My role at Strategyzer is senior product designer, and my remit is the product. The website, sales decks and client reports sit outside it. This piece covers why I took them on anyway, how I built the system, and what it has done for the business.

Design work nobody owned

Strategyzer's product team is small: two developers, a head of product and me. I was hired to design the product, and that is where my time is meant to go. Everything else a customer sees, from web pages to sales decks to client reports, had no designer attached to it.

Each team had found its own way around that.

Marketing brought in a freelance designer for every new page on the website. Each page came with a brief, rounds of revisions, a cost and a wait.

Sales built decks in PowerPoint without design support. Getting a deck to a standard the team was proud of took three to four days, and in sales that time is expensive. A lead who waits most of a week for a proposal is a lead that cools.

Customer delivery and client relations produced reports for external clients. Consultants whose real value is strategic insight were spending hours on formatting and on deciding how to present data, and any inconsistency went straight to a paying client.

None of this was a failure on anyone's part. These teams had the expertise and the content. What they lacked was a design system built for the way they work. I could see the gap clearly, and my background in design and hands-on work with AI put me in a good position to close it.

Encoding design judgment

A design system usually means tokens, components and a well-kept Figma library. That is valuable for designers and does very little for a sales rep with a proposal due on Friday. A component library doesn't assemble itself into a deck.

The harder thing to hand over is judgment: the many small decisions a designer makes almost automatically. Which layout suits this content, when a chart communicates better than a table, how much weight a heading needs, what belongs in a hero and where it sits. If these teams were going to produce design-quality work without a designer, the system had to make those calls for them.

Claude skills made that practical. A skill is a packaged set of instructions and assets that Claude loads whenever it does a particular kind of work, which let me capture how our brand behaves as well as how it looks. Guidelines such as "Regular weight everywhere, Bold only for chips" or "full-bleed primary-color heroes with the illustration on the right" became defaults Claude applies every time.

I had already proven the approach on our product UI, where I turned our FondUI design system into a self-contained skill extracted directly from Figma. The open question was whether it would hold up for teams who had never opened Figma.

Three skills, built around three outputs

I built a separate skill for each team, designed around the thing that team ships.

Marketing: web pages

The marketing skill contains our full website design system: brand assets, the TWK Everett typeface, semantic tokens, component rules and layout patterns. The team writes the content, hands it to Claude and gets back a page that follows the system from the first draft, with no freelancer brief required.

Sales: decks

The sales skill encodes how a Strategyzer deck should look and flow, from slide layouts and visual hierarchy to how a story builds for a prospect. A rep brings the content, and Claude produces a polished deck in about thirty minutes. Reps now turn around several decks a day.

Client delivery: reports

The reporting skill governs how findings and data are structured and visualized for external clients. The delivery and client relations teams no longer spend time on layout or on how a figure should be presented, so their attention goes to the substance of what the client needs to know.

How I built it

Each skill is a self-contained package, and building them was as much a systems problem as a design one. A few principles run through all of them.

A single source of truth. For the product system, I extracted components and tokens directly from Figma using MCP tooling, so the skill stays in step with the design files. [?] Were the marketing, sales and reports skills built the same way?

Semantic tokens. Color, type, spacing and components are organized in semantic hierarchies and exposed as kebab-case CSS custom properties. Claude selects each value by its role in the layout, which keeps output consistent across every page, deck and report.

Embedded brand assets. Brand assets and our TWK Everett fonts ship inside the skills, so Claude never substitutes a generic default.

Explicit rules. Type weights, hero layouts, navigation order and component usage are written as firm rules. Loose guidance is where AI output starts to drift, so I kept room for interpretation to a minimum.

Versioning. Each skill is maintained centrally and versioned; the marketing system is currently on v2.3. When a rule changes, I update it once and every team picks it up in their next session.

The impact

The most visible change is speed. Work that used to wait on a freelancer or a multi-day PowerPoint effort now moves as fast as the content is ready.

Quality improved alongside speed. Decks, pages and reports are more polished, the language is sharper, and the layouts are cleaner and more consistent. A prospect reading a sales deck and a client reading a report now see the same Strategyzer.

For sales, the time saved compounds into revenue. Reps send more decks, reach more leads and get proposals in front of prospects while interest is still high, which means a larger pipeline that moves faster. The work also changed my role. I'm still a senior product designer and I still own the product. Alongside that, I now act as design lead for the wider company, responsible for how design shows up wherever Strategyzer meets a customer. I took that on by choice, and it has become one of the most rewarding parts of my work.

What I took away

  1. Internal teams deserve the same design rigor as customers. Treating marketing, sales and delivery as users, each with their own jobs and constraints, shaped every decision in these skills.
  2. Judgment is harder to hand over than assets. The library was the easy part. The real work was writing down the decisions I make instinctively.
  3. Constraints build trust. The stricter the rules, the more consistent the output, and the more readily teams relied on it.
  4. A system needs an owner. Versioning, feedback and regular updates are what keep the skills useful as the business changes.
  5. Business terms carry the argument. Hours saved and deals moved made the case for this work far better than "it looks more consistent" ever could.

Beyond the brief

The most valuable thing I designed this year isn't a screen in our product. It's a system that lets people across Strategyzer produce design-quality work without a designer in the room.

It's also a fair picture of how I work. I look for the places where design can move the business, then build what's needed to get there, including when that sits outside my job description.

If you're working on a similar problem, or building a team that needs design to reach well beyond the product, I'd love to talk.
